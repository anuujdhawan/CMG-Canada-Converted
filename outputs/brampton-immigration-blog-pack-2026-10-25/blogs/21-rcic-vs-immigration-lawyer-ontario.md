## Quick answer

In Ontario, both a properly licensed RCIC and a lawyer who is a member in good standing of a provincial or territorial law society may provide immigration advice within their authorized scope. Some Ontario paralegals may also handle permitted immigration matters. Choose based on the work required, verify the professional’s licence, and get the scope and fee in writing.

A Regulated Canadian Immigration Consultant (RCIC) and a lawyer are different professional designations, and both may be authorized to provide immigration advice when properly licensed. In Ontario, some paralegals may also be authorized for immigration matters within their permitted scope. The right person depends on the issue, the work you need, and the professional’s current licence and experience.

IRCC says immigration consultants must be members of the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants, while lawyers must belong to a provincial or territorial law society. Ontario paralegals must be members of the Law Society of Ontario. Use the government’s [authorized-representative checker](https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/services/immigration-citizenship-representative/choose/authorized.html) and the regulator’s [public register](https://register.college-ic.ca/) to verify the person you are considering.

## Match the professional to the work

For a routine permit or permanent-residence application, ask whether the representative regularly handles the relevant program and what services are included. If the matter involves Federal Court judicial review, litigation, or another legal proceeding, ask whether a lawyer with the right experience is needed or should be involved.

Do not select someone solely because they use “lawyer,” “consultant,” or “expert” in an advertisement. Confirm their full legal name, licence status, good standing, disciplinary information, and whether the person you verify is the same person who will do the work.

Before paying, ask for a written service agreement and itemized scope. It should identify fees, responsibilities, communication, and how deadlines will be managed. A valid licence does not guarantee a successful application, but it helps you confirm who is accountable under the regulator’s rules.

## A practical way to compare representatives

Start by describing the work you need: a temporary-resident application, permanent-residence file, sponsorship, refusal review, admissibility issue, or court proceeding. Ask the professional how often they handle that kind of matter, who will prepare the file, and what work is included after submission. If a court proceeding such as judicial review may be involved, ask whether a lawyer with relevant court experience is necessary.

Verify the individual, not just the company name. For an RCIC, check the College of Immigration and Citizenship Consultants public register and confirm that the person’s status permits them to practise. For a lawyer, check the law society in the province or territory where they are licensed. For an Ontario paralegal, check the Law Society of Ontario register and the person’s permitted scope. Make sure the name on the agreement matches the person you verified.

Then compare the written service agreements. It should state the representative’s identity, the exact work, professional fees, separate disbursements, communication expectations, and how deadlines and additional requests will be handled. Ask how you will receive a copy of the final application and submission confirmation.

A licence is an accountability check, not a promise of approval. Be cautious if someone refuses to identify the person doing the work, guarantees a visa or PR result, or asks you to sign forms you have not reviewed. Keep the agreement, receipts, and copies of all documents. For a Brampton applicant, these same verification steps apply whether the consultation is in person or online.

Court proceedings have separate legal requirements. Ask a lawyer qualified in the relevant court process to assess whether they should handle the matter.
