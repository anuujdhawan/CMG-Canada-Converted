- **Work with any employer** — short-term work to fund travel, with a limit on how long you can stay with any single employer (a design rule that keeps the visa about travel, not employment).
- **Study for up to four months.**

3. **Overstaying and conditions drift.** The visa's conditions are conditions; breaches follow you into every future Australian application — including the skilled ones you might want later.
4. **The insurance assumption.** Health cover isn't just wise; some agreements require it, and Australia's medical costs are not budget-travel-friendly.

- **Test the job market in your profession.** A year of Australian work contacts — even in mixed roles — tells you whether sponsorship or skilled migration is realistic, and in which cities.
- **Build Australian experience deliberately.** Work in (or adjacent to) your skilled occupation where possible; experience and references accumulate.
- **Meet employers who sponsor.** Australia's employers hire working holidaymakers constantly; the ones who value you become 482-sponsorship conversations.
- **Scout the regional play.** Specified regional work for the second year doubles as a live test of the 491 regional strategy — regional towns that suit working holidays are often the same regions nominating skilled migrants.
- **Points homework in the background.** English test sitting, skills assessment groundwork — the quiet admin that positions you to lodge an EOI before the 417 ends.

3. **Vague treatment plans.** "Consultations in Australia" without named specialists, facilities, or appointments.
4. **The temporary-intent tension.** Cases where the medical plan is open-ended and the return case is weak need careful framing — the visa can accommodate treatment timelines, but the case must be honest about them.

**Where 300 applications actually fail:**

1. **The wedding exists only on paper.** No booked venue, no notice lodged, no family who knows — the intention-to-marry evidence is thin, and the genuineness finding follows.
2. **The meeting-history gap.** Couples who haven't met, or whose meetings can't be evidenced — the requirement is bright-line.
3. **Sponsorship-limitation surprises.** A sponsor with prior partner sponsorships discovering the spacing rules after the application is built around them.
4. **The clock mismanagement.** Couples who marry late in the window, or drift past it — the transition application then has problems the visa's design didn't intend. Marry early in the stay; the partner application deserves maximum runway.
5. **Non-disclosure.** Prior relationships and refusals, as always — disclosed honestly and early, they're manageable; discovered later, they're not.

**The strategic comparison: 300 vs. offshore partner (309).** Couples often ask whether to marry abroad first and apply for the 309, or use the 300 to marry in Australia:

- **The 309 route** suits couples who can and want to marry at home — one application, the two-year arc, no wedding deadline in a foreign jurisdiction.
- **The 300 route** suits couples who want (or need) to marry in Australia — including same-sex couples from countries where marriage isn't available to them, a genuinely important use of the visa.
- **The honest factors:** where you want to marry, whose families attend, the processing-time calculus for each, and the evidence you already hold. Both roads reach permanent residence; the right one is the one that matches your actual plans.

**The practical playbook:**

1. **Build the relationship file continuously** — the 300 assesses the relationship, not just the engagement.
2. **Make the wedding real early** — bookings and notices that predate and postdate the application.
3. **Marry within the window — early** — then lodge the partner application with the full evidence file.
4. **Plan the interim status** — work and study rights on the 300, and the transition's timing, should be understood before you fly.
